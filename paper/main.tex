\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Unpaired transport models such as the sliced-Wasserstein autoencoder (SWAE) learn a
stochastic map between truth-level and detector-level four-vectors from marginals
alone. Marginal agreement, however, does not establish that the model has learned the
response: a deterministic map can absorb the detector resolution into its mean and
still match every one-dimensional projection. We train a single mass-blind SWAE on
real CMS 2012 open data together with a theory prior for the $J/\psi$ and $Z$
resonances, and ask three questions. First, is the response identifiable - does the
learned stochastic channel carry the resolution? Second, does the same object transfer
to the $\Upsilon(1S,2S,3S)$ family, which is excluded from training? Third, does it
invert per event? We answer the first with a fixed-truth repeated-decoding budget and
a scorecard that reports every gauge against its identity and finite-sample floor, and
we show that noise-aware checkpoint selection picks the deterministic solution in
eight of eight arms. Fixing the noise amplitude from an independent resolution
measurement restores the channel: the $\Upsilon(1S)$ intra-event width becomes
86.5-91.6 MeV against the 84 MeV CMS resolution. The same model predicts the
$\Upsilon$ peak positions to $+21.6/+2.9/+6.3$ MeV with a best-to-last drift of
1-3 MeV and a systematic budget dominated by the checkpoint choice (85-92 MeV), while
the peak widths remain over-spread (290-299 MeV) and per-event unfolding improves on
the identity map but does not reach the oracle. We report the refuted mechanisms (a
stronger learned noise amplitude, a cycle-consistent noise schedule, a heavier
per-event weight) as constraints, and we state explicitly what a claim of learned
detector response requires.

\section{Introduction}

Fast, data-driven detector simulation and unfolding are central applications of
machine learning in high-energy physics\textsuperscript{\cite{otus_paper,calogan}}.
The OTUS/SWAE formulation\textsuperscript{\cite{otus_paper,swae,wae}} learns an
unpaired transport between a physically interpretable truth space $\mathcal{Z}$ and
detector-level observations $\mathcal{X}$ with a sliced-Wasserstein objective,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
= \mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim E(x)}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde x\sim D(z)}\|x-\tilde x\|^2
+ \lambda\, d_{\mathrm{SW}}\!\left(p_E(z),p(z)\right),
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the encoder (unfolding) and $D$ the decoder (simulation).

The literal objective is a \emph{marginal} criterion. Our starting observation, which
motivates this paper, is that marginal agreement is not evidence of a learned
response: because the latent ensemble varies in coordinates correlated with the
resonance mass, a deterministic decoder can reproduce the observed mass spread while
its stochastic channel collapses, and every one-dimensional projection still closes.
We turn that observation into three questions and answer each with a measurement
rather than a metric:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Identification (RQ1).} Does the stochastic channel carry the detector
        resolution, or is the resolution manufactured by the mean map?
  \item \textbf{Transfer (RQ2).} Does one shared, mass-blind response trained on
        $J/\psi$ and $Z$ predict a resonance that never entered training - the
        $\Upsilon(1S,2S,3S)$ family - in both peak position and width?
  \item \textbf{Inversion (RQ3).} Does the same trained object unfold per event, with
        calibrated uncertainty, where paired truth exists?
\end{itemize}

Related work places this in a crowded landscape. For unfolding, OmniFold is the
current reference\textsuperscript{\cite{omnifold}}, and discriminative reweighting
without iterations (AUSSIE, arXiv:2602.24282) and Wasserstein-loss unfolding with an
explicit injectivity condition (arXiv:2603.20903) are the closest modern
alternatives; both are used here as external reference points rather than as
baselines we claim to beat. The immediate predecessor of this work is the OTUS
autoencoder\textsuperscript{\cite{otus_paper}}, which established that an unpaired
transport can be learned between theory-level and detector-level samples without
any $(z,x)$ pairs. Its two demonstrations, however, generated \textbf{both} sides with
MadGraph5, Pythia8 and Delphes: the detector it learned was itself a simulation, so
the response could be checked against the generator that produced it. This paper
replaces that simulated detector level with real CMS open data and therefore has no
such oracle. CWola inverted detector effects with conditional generative
networks\textsuperscript{\cite{cwola}}, and the unpaired line continues with
point-cloud translation\textsuperscript{\cite{unpairedpointcloud}}.
On the simulation side, detector-level generative
modelling has moved from GANs\textsuperscript{\cite{calogan,caloflow}} to flows,
diffusion and particle-cloud models\textsuperscript{\cite{calochallenge}}; the
neural-optimal-transport line\textsuperscript{\cite{cuturi_sinkhorn,genevay_sinkhorn}}
and flow matching\textsuperscript{\cite{lipman_flow,albergo_stochastic}} supply the
building blocks we reuse. What is missing across that literature, and what this
paper supplies, is a criterion for deciding whether an unpaired model has learned
the response at all.

The work makes three moves. (i) \textbf{Real detector data.} The detector level is CMS
2012 open data rather than a fast simulation, so the learned response is the real
detector's and there is no generator to validate against. (ii) \textbf{A different network.}
Encoder and decoder are cylindrical-coordinate residual flows with an explicit
per-event noise channel (a Gaussian core and a Student-$t$ tail), conditioned on muon
kinematics only, and trained jointly on $J/\psi$ and $Z$ with one shared, mass-blind
parameter set. (iii) \textbf{A criterion and a measurement.} An identification scorecard
that asks whether the resolution is carried by the noise or faked by the mean map,
the demonstration that noise-aware marginal selection is actively harmful (eight of
eight arms), a frozen-amplitude calibration and what it buys, a zero-shot transfer
test to the $\Upsilon$ family that separates what transfers (peak positions) from what
does not (widths, conditional shape), and a quantified systematics budget with the
refuted mechanisms.

\subsection{The stochastic channel is not identified by marginal objectives}

With a learned noise amplitude the channel collapses: the tail scale falls from
$8.1\times10^{-4}$ to $1.75\times10^{-4}$ and the core scale sits on its floor, while
the late-training $\Upsilon$ shift ($-125/-136/-136$ MeV) is unchanged when the noise
multipliers are set to 0, 0.25, 0.5 or 1.0, and unchanged with all noise off. The
resolution therefore lives in the deterministic mean map. Scoring validation at the
checkpoint's native noise - the intuitive remedy - selects the deterministic stage-one
warmup in eight of eight arms, because the score is a marginal gauge and a stochastic
checkpoint scores worse at its own operating point.

\centering

give 10.5-11.0 MeV. Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} summarises the resulting scorecard,

\section{Discussion}

Marginal closure is necessary but not sufficient; the identification scorecard is our
proposal for what to report instead, and the systematics budget shows how to read a
transfer claim once the selection rule is declared. The response is demonstrated for a
fixed two-particle final state, no theory or detector systematic uncertainty is
propagated, and the refuted mechanisms (a stronger learned noise amplitude, a
cycle-consistent noise schedule, a heavier per-event weight) constrain the explanation
without yet providing the missing ingredient: an objective that ties the resolution to
the noise channel while leaving the mean map identifiable.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data and selection}
Detector-level data are CMS 2012 open-data opposite-sign dimuons, selected in the
$J/\psi$ and $Z$ windows; truth-level events are generated with MadGraph5 + Pythia8
and passed through the same fiducial selection. The two samples are never paired
event-by-event. The $\Upsilon$ sample is held out: it is never used for training,
tuning, checkpoint selection or metric design, and is opened only after the model is
frozen. Locked splits \textnormal{[measured]}: the legacy-prior group provides
2,899,563 detector-level and 71,659 truth-level $J/\psi$ events, and 4,207,696
detector-level and 800,000 truth-level $Z$ events; the honest component-aware group
provides 501,317 truth-level $J/\psi$ and 401,718 truth-level $Z$ events over the same
detector-level stream. \todo{Selection tables, split manifest, cache fingerprints.}

\subsection{Priors}
The $J/\psi$ and $Z$ priors are component-aware mixtures (signal plus continuum). Two
regimes exist and must be quoted with every in-domain number: a legacy hand-smeared
$J/\psi$ prior, whose required additional smearing is 12.2 MeV, and the honest
component-aware prior, whose requirement is 30.0 MeV - a factor 2.46. A
sideband-derived continuum-reweighted $\Upsilon$ prior is the primary transfer
readout.

\subsection{Model}
Encoder and decoder are short residual flows in cylindrical coordinates, sharing one
mass-blind parameter set; at each of $K$ steps a physics-conditioned network predicts
a bounded mean residual, a Gaussian core scale and a Student-$t$ tail scale, with
azimuthal transport on $S^1$ and energies reconstructed on the muon mass shell. The
response is written as $x = M(z) + \epsilon$ with $\epsilon \sim
p_\theta(\epsilon\mid z)$. The trained joint arms carry 830,710 parameters (415,355
per direction) in four hidden layers of 256 units with two flow steps, i.e. 3.32 MB of
float32 weights \textnormal{[measured]}.

\subsection{What changed relative to the predecessor}

Three changes distinguish this model from the OTUS autoencoder
\textsuperscript{\cite{otus_paper}}, all source-verified in the repository:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Data.} The detector level is CMS 2012 open data, not a Delphes
        sample, so the response has a real, irreducible resolution and no oracle.
  \item \textbf{Network.} Instead of a plain multilayer autoencoder, both maps are
        short residual flows in cylindrical coordinates: per step a
        physics-conditioned network predicts a bounded mean residual and the two
        noise scales, azimuth is transported on $S^1$, and energies are
        reconstructed on the muon mass shell.
  \item \textbf{Conditioning and scope.} The maps are conditioned on muon
        kinematics only (the explicit pair-mass coordinate is excluded and the
        exclusion is enforced by a contract test), and one shared parameter set is
        trained jointly on $J/\psi$ and $Z$ with balanced per-region statistics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Objective}
The loss combines the paired reconstruction cycle $D(E(x))$, a latent prior term on
$E(x)$, a direct generator term $D(z)$, a $\pt$-sliced conditional mass term, and a
z-space consistency cycle
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{z} = \big\| \mathrm{std}(z) - \mathrm{std}(E(D(z))) \big\|^2 ,
\end{equation}
which asks the composition of the two maps to be the identity on the truth side; the
x-side cycle alone can be satisfied by a deterministic autoencoder.

\subsection{Identification diagnostics}
For a fixed truth event we decode repeatedly and measure the intra-event width; the
exact split $\mathrm{Var}(x) = \mathbb{E}_z[\mathrm{Var}(x\mid z)] +
\mathrm{Var}_z[\mathbb{E}(x\mid z)]$ separates the noise share from the mean-map
share. The response gate requires the intra-event width to lie within 0.8-1.25 of the
required resolution and $\sigma_{\mathrm{only}} \geq 50$ MeV on the $\Upsilon$ states;
a checkpoint is flagged degenerate when the ratio falls below 0.5. Conditional closure
is scored per equal-count pair-$\pt$ slice as $W_1$ over its finite-sample floor.

\subsection{Checkpoint selection}
Selection is a tiered rule rather than a weighted sum: reject any checkpoint whose
response gate fails or that is degenerate, rank the survivors by conditional closure
and then by the in-domain score, and report identification failure explicitly rather
than crowning a marginal winner. The transfer axis is report-only and provably cannot
enter the key.

\subsection{Systematics and statistics}
Four axes are quantified for each claim: the evaluation prior, the checkpoint, the
subsample seed, and a bootstrap of the decoded sample. The reported spreads are the
half-ranges of each axis; the bootstrap is the statistical floor, not a systematic. A
training-seed axis requires new runs and is not yet measured.
